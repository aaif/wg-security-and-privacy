- **Kill switch presentation** — Marc from MintID presented a revocation protocol for agents on the open internet. MintID is a non-profit foundation that stewards a chain intended as a KYC-backed identity and accountability layer for agents, and ZATQ is the first validator, operator, and vetted issuer. A person completes biometric KYC with the issuer, receives credentials, and hands a public key to an agent through a control surface. When a service asks the agent to prove a verified person stands behind it, the agent answers with a zero-knowledge proof bound to the request and valid for ten seconds, and the verifier checks it against the issuer's latest status on the chain, returning a KYC tier from one to four and whether an escrow account backs liability. The person's identity never lands on the chain and is disclosed only through due process. Three revocation paths exist: a kill switch pulled by the agent runtime or by the user with a revocation credential, a cascade that nullifies every agent under a user's root authority, and an issuer-level master kill switch. Testing in the Identity and Trust WG showed a 230 to 245 second bound for all chain nodes to lock out a rogue agent, and the single production node revokes in 28 to 33 seconds. A verifier has 60 seconds after a kill switch to remove the credential or its new proofs are rejected. Marc stressed the scope is deliberately narrow: identity attestation for agents with a liable person behind them on the open internet, not enterprise perimeters where SPIFFE and service accounts already apply. He also asked whether the central identity provider named in the credential revocation containment of the group's kill switch design pattern means an enterprise provider or one for the open internet, which Alex will take offline.
- **Kill switch discussion** — Govindaraj asked how the protocol aligns with OpenID's Continuous Access Evaluation Profile (CAEP), the Shared Signals Framework, RFC 7009 token revocation, back-channel logout, and the transaction tokens drafts, and noted that the OpenAI and Anthropic people he spoke with at AgentCon rely heavily on OpenID plumbing. He recommended Marc write the protocol up as an IETF draft for an internet of agents, offered to check the IETF tracker for existing kill switch work, and suggested the kill switch could become an event fed back to MCP servers now that MCP is adding events and tasks, blocking a flagged agent at authentication time. Marc clarified the agent can self-revoke, and Govindaraj said both self-revocation and third-party revocation are needed. Alex Khimich pointed out that CAEP requires the authorization server to be reachable, so a DNS failure or hyperscaler outage leaves nobody able to check revocation status, and a ledger lets every participant hold a copy of the revocation state. Govindaraj agreed that gap could inform the next CAEP revision. Marc said a public ledger is unavoidable if trust is to rest on several attested institutions and never on a single foundation, and that liability is the link he most wants to solve.
